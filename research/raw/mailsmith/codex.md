# Mailsmith: research for Sweetbriar

**Research date: 2026-10-11.** Historical observations below retain their original dates. Current product, library and platform statements were checked on this research date; they are documentation findings, not hands-on compatibility tests.

The backbone is the supplied **Mailsmith 2.2 User Manual**, copyright 2009 **Stickshift Software, LLC**, distributed in the supplied 2.3.1 image. I read all 217 pages. “Manual p. N” means the supplied `=== Page N ===` marker, **not the printed page number**. The help book has older Bare Bones copyright dates and sometimes describes superseded behavior. Later release notes take precedence when they explicitly change a feature. [Manual pp. 1–4](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [help title](codex-artifacts/sources/help/mailsmith_title.html).

**Evidence labels:** “documented” means a primary source describes the behavior; reviews establish the writer’s experience; reminiscences establish recollection. Recommendations are proposals. Missing documentation does not prove a feature’s absence.

## 1. TL;DR

- **Mailsmith was a Mac email client built around serious text editing.** Bare Bones shipped 1.0 on **1998-05-04**; its BBEdit-derived editor, programmable workflow and distributed filters were its distinguishing combination. [Bare Bones history](https://www.barebones.com/company/history.html); [archived editing page, 2003-08-01](https://web.archive.org/web/20030801213043id_/http://www.barebones.com:80/products/mailsmith/editing.shtml).
- The 2009 successor was **Stickshift Software**, owned by Bare Bones founder Rich Siegel—not “Stick Software.” Version 2.2 became freeware on **2009-08-18**. Freeware did not mean an open-source release. [Transfer announcement](https://www.barebones.com/company/press/mailsmith-transfer_pr.html); [Manual pp. 3–4](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).
- **The end is verified:** latest published release **2.4.3, 2018-05-14**; the vendor says development is inactive and there will be no 64-bit upgrade. Apple stopped supporting 32-bit applications in Catalina 10.15. [Vendor updates](http://www.mailsmith.org/support/mailsmith/updates.html); [Apple’s compatibility explanation](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/103076).
- Revive **quote-aware reflow, six-clipboard-style history, append-copy, split views, regex replacement, reusable clippings, private searchable notes, keyboard filing and inspectable automation**. These are documented capabilities, not nostalgic guesses. [Manual pp. 39, 47–64, 177–195](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).
- **Distributed filtering deserves a modern adaptation.** Reusable rules belonged to mailbox contexts; Deposit, Transfer and Copy had different control-flow semantics. Alphabetical mailbox order could change the result. Sweetbriar should use explicit order, stable identifiers and a preview of each rule’s effects. [Manual pp. 94–116](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).
- **Do not promise Mailsmith’s speed or reliability as historical facts.** Contemporary reviewers reported slow storage, crashes and recovery concerns, even while loving its editor and control. [ATPM, January 1999](https://atpm.com/5.01/page8.shtml); [TidBITS, 2003-07-28](https://tidbits.com/2003/07/28/true-confessions-of-a-mailsmith-switcher/).
- The heritage is **partly surviving**: Apple Mail has scripted rules; Thunderbird has filters and saved searches; Spark and Mimestream have templates with variables; MailMate carries a particularly strong text-first lineage. Sweetbriar needs better integration and safety, not a claim that all competitors lack power features. [Apple](https://support.apple.com/en-mide/guide/mail/mlhlp1120/mac); [Thunderbird](https://support.mozilla.org/en-US/kb/using-saved-searches); [Spark](https://sparkmailapp.com/help/spark-ai/templates-ai); [Mimestream](https://mimestream.com/); [MailMate](https://manual.mailmate-app.com/).
- **Recommendation:** a native AppKit text editor over a reusable Rust core, with plain-text and optional Markdown composition, safe HTML reading, one shared query/rule model, and local durability independent of the search index. **Strongest counterargument:** this serves a demanding niche and can recreate Mailsmith’s complexity before Sweetbriar has mastered everyday account synchronization.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 History, business, platforms and ending

The name emerged from the earlier **Bluto** project. The supported historical trajectory runs from classic Mac OS to native Mac OS X, then Universal PowerPC/Intel builds, finally Intel-only builds. A Windows or Linux Mailsmith release was not verified. [Bare Bones history](https://www.barebones.com/company/history.html); [archived 1.5 features, 2003-01-11](https://web.archive.org/web/20030111060025id_/http://www.barebones.com:80/products/mailsmith/features.shtml); [2.4 release notes](http://www.mailsmith.org/support/mailsmith/current_notes.html).

The transfer preserved an important personal connection: Rich Siegel owned Stickshift while remaining Bare Bones’ CEO. Stickshift’s company page says it incorporated in Rhode Island in **June 2009**. The announcement framed Mailsmith as a personal project and Bare Bones as Siegel’s first priority. This supports a limited-resources explanation; it does **not** establish sales, profitability or staff counts. [Transfer announcement](https://www.barebones.com/company/press/mailsmith-transfer_pr.html); [Stickshift company page](http://www.mailsmith.org/company/).

The manual credits past engineering to **Jim Correia, Dave Hill, Jon Hueras, Steve Kalkwarf, Rich Siegel and Steve Sisak**, with Stickshift responsible for the then-current design/engineering and Seth Dillingham providing additional engineering. Documentation credits include John Gruber and William Porter. This is evidence of contributors, not evidence that an entire team transferred in 2009. The later employment of every contributor, disposition of the complete source tree, and any subsequent code sale are **unverified**. The manual identifies licensed **NeoAccess** technology and a restrictive software license; no public source release was verified. [Manual pp. 2–4](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

**Why it ended:** the decisive documented barrier is the developer’s explicit refusal to undertake 64-bit modernization. As checked **2026-10-11**, downloads remain available but one-to-one technical support is not offered. Apple’s current page identifies **macOS 27 Golden Gate** and Apple-silicon compatibility; its security notice dates release to **2026-09-14**. Mailsmith cannot run as an ordinary native application on that target. [Vendor home](http://www.mailsmith.org/); [support](http://www.mailsmith.org/support/); [Apple 32-bit policy](https://support.apple.com/en-ie/103076); [Golden Gate compatibility](https://support.apple.com/en-ca/127255); [release/security notice](https://support.apple.com/en-us/149035).

**Interpretation:** POP-only retrieval, little adaptation to HTML-heavy correspondence and dependence on a local archive made expansion harder as users expected multiple synchronized devices. Those are plausible disadvantages, not a verified commercial cause of death. A devoted minority continued to prefer them: Glenn Fleishman was still using Mailsmith in **2016** despite acknowledging POP, absent threading and slow search. [Macworld retrospective, 2016-06-27](https://www.macworld.com/article/228272/in-defense-of-old-mac-software.html).

The archives were actually fetched: Bare Bones’ [2003 feature page](https://web.archive.org/web/20030111060025id_/http://www.barebones.com:80/products/mailsmith/features.shtml), [editing page](https://web.archive.org/web/20030801213043id_/http://www.barebones.com:80/products/mailsmith/editing.shtml) and [filtering page](https://web.archive.org/web/20030620135905id_/http://www.barebones.com:80/products/mailsmith/filtering.shtml), plus mailsmith.org snapshots from [2011-07-21](https://web.archive.org/web/20110721050818id_/http://www.mailsmith.org:80/) and [2016-01-02](https://web.archive.org/web/20160102011253id_/http://www.mailsmith.org:80/). The last two advertise 2.3.1 and 2.4 respectively. HTTPS certificate failures on the live Mailsmith and preserved MacTech sites were worked around using HTTP.

### 2.2 Feature inventory: what the documentation actually establishes

Unless qualified below, this inventory describes **the 2009 2.2 manual**, not a claim that every command existed in 1.0 or remains unchanged in 2.4.3.

#### Accounts, composition and mail transport

Mailsmith retrieved mail with **POP3** and sent with **SMTP**. It supported multiple named accounts, a default account, per-account sender identity and signatures, and reply-account selection by matching the original recipient address. “Personalities” was not verified as a Mailsmith command or distinct identity model; the documented mechanism is accounts. [Manual pp. 13, 26–30, 34](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Account controls included checking at startup or intervals; inclusion in global checks independently of periodic checking; leaving mail on the server or deleting it after an age threshold; downloading only a partial message above a size limit; **Get from Server**, **Re-Fetch All** and password-changing controls. SMTP settings included separate credentials, authentication methods, configurable ports and negotiated or required SSL/TLS. Historical options included APOP, CRAM-MD5, LOGIN and PLAIN; these are inventory items, not recommended authentication defaults for 2026. Additional RFC 822 headers were configurable. The 2.2 custom `X-Mailer` option was explicitly removed in 2.3. [Manual pp. 27–30](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [2.3 changes](http://www.mailsmith.org/support/mailsmith/arch_notes_23.html).

| Area | Concrete behavior |
|---|---|
| Composition panels | Recipients, options, enclosures and **Notes**. Notes were searchable with Advanced Query and **never transmitted**. [Manual pp. 33–39](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt) |
| Keyboard navigation | Address completion with arrow-key alternatives; Return adds To, Shift-Return CC, Shift-Option-Return BCC. Within the body, Tab inserts a tab; Option-Tab cycles fields and Control-Tab cycles panels. [Manual pp. 35–36](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt) |
| Recipients and priority | Copy Sender by BCC; Suppress Recipient List moves recipients to BCC and puts the sender in To; five priorities from Urgent to Bulk. [Manual p. 37](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt) |
| Replies | Reply, Reply All, Reply with Stationery; quote a selected passage or the whole message; configurable attribution and insertion position. [Manual pp. 40–41, 170–171](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt) |
| Other reuse | Forward inline or as a MIME attachment; Redirect retaining the original sender presentation with a forwarding-account annotation; Re-Send creates a new composition with original settings and recipients. [Manual pp. 40–41, 171](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt) |
| Signatures | Per-account/per-message signatures; random selection, with separate eligibility for random use and menu inclusion; signature imports and XML storage. [Manual pp. 42–44](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt) |
| Draft/send lifecycle | Save Draft produces an editable draft; Send Now and Queue for Delivery were distinct choices, also exposed through modifier keys. **Queued messages could be deleted but not edited.** [Manual pp. 43–44](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt) |
| Style and mistake checks | Warnings for excessive quotation, quoted signatures, minimal added response, URLs without angle brackets, missing subject, large messages or many recipients. [Manual pp. 41–42, 173–174](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt) |

Attachments could be dragged in, pasted from file references or images, and created from folders as ZIP archives. Text files could be opened for editing rather than attached, depending on a preference; file contents, paths and folder listings could be inserted into the body. Historical attachment encodings included **Base64, AppleDouble and BinHex**; optional StuffIt compression and separately installed PGP software supported additional workflows. Mailsmith’s encryption UI was an integration with another application, not proof of a built-in modern cryptographic implementation. [Manual pp. 37–39, 77–78](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

#### The BBEdit-derived editor

The archived vendor page expressly advertises BBEdit’s editing/transformation power. Macworld’s 2.0 review identifies the shared text engine. The manual gives the operational detail: this was substantially more than a plain-text toggle. [Archived editing page](https://web.archive.org/web/20030801213043id_/http://www.barebones.com:80/products/mailsmith/editing.shtml); [Macworld, 2003-10-31](https://www.macworld.com/article/168357/mailsmith2.html).

The combination mattered for actual correspondence: collect excerpts from several messages, paste them quoted, split a long reply so the source passage remains visible, reflow each quotation level, and use regex replacement to clean repeated boilerplate. That workflow is an **interpretation grounded in the documented commands**, not a claimed preset macro.

#### Reading, mailboxes and keyboard workflow

Mailsmith offered a three-pane **Mail Browser**, a separate Mailbox List, individual mailbox windows and standalone message windows. Mailboxes could nest without a documented fixed depth limit. Standard mailboxes were incoming, outgoing, drafts, trash, errors and spam. Unread counts included descendant information; columns could be resized, rearranged and configured by window/mailbox, with sender/recipient presentation depending on message direction. [Manual pp. 69–75](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Spacebar paged through the body and then advanced to the next message. **Move Messages to Mailbox** offered keyboard selection by typing; drag filing, labels, priorities, flags and read/replied/forwarded/redirected/sent indicators supported organization. There were distinct trash, permanent-delete and empty-trash commands. **Copy Item Link** generated message URLs for use outside Mailsmith; **Move Duplicates to Trash** was documented, although its duplicate-detection algorithm was not established here. [Manual pp. 76, 78–80, 199–202](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Reading was intentionally text-first: choose a MIME plain-text alternative when present, otherwise translate HTML into text; explicitly open HTML in an external browser when necessary. Image/movie attachments used separate viewing windows in the older documentation. **Open Digest** turned a MIME digest into an individually navigable/replyable message list. Received-message text could be transformed temporarily for readability, but ordinary view edits were **not saved back to the received message**. Full headers and **Show Message Source** supported inspection. [Manual pp. 75–78](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

The **POP Monitor** was unusually transparent: inspect a server/account separately, obtain full information or sizes, download selected messages, save them as mbox, and mark messages for deletion on disconnect. It even documented saving malformed messages that normal parsing could not handle. [Manual pp. 81–82](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

#### Search: precision, not merely a search box

Two systems shared some syntax but served different purposes. **Find/Replace** searched the editing buffer: Find Again, reverse search, case/whole-word controls, wraparound, use-selection-for-find/replace, Replace All and Replace & Find. **Simple Query** and **Advanced Query** searched stored mail, with chosen mailbox scopes and optional descendants; results used mailbox/message views. [Manual pp. 117–122](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Advanced Query reused filter criteria, including headers, body, addresses, dates, account, status and **private Notes**. Literal, **PCRE-based “grep”**, and approximate text matching were documented; fuzzy matching exposed a **20–100%** setting. Address comparisons could target display name, complete address, user ID or domain. The PCRE chapter documents captures/backreferences, named groups, lookarounds, atomic grouping, conditionals, recursive patterns and replacement case conversion. This is not the same as invoking a Unix `grep` process. [Manual pp. 103–106, 120–153](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Important limits: condition combinations were evaluated **left to right**, without arbitrary parenthesized Boolean groups; `or else` meant XOR. The manual also warned that pathological regexes could freeze the application. It would be a mistake to revive either limitation as a compatibility virtue. [Manual pp. 104–105, 152](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

#### Distributed filters: a mailbox hierarchy as a processing structure

Filters could be created from a message, from scratch, or alongside a mailbox. The same filter could attach to several mailboxes; attached filters had their own order and enable switches. A simple mailbox-creation filter used **Transfer**, so not every convenient filter-creation workflow was distributed filtering. [Manual pp. 88–94, 115–116](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Incoming acted as the logical starting context; mailbox sibling order was alphabetical. Matching a branch could exclude later siblings and continue down its children. Consequently, a mailbox rename could change processing. The crucial actions were: [Manual pp. 94–99](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

| Action | Effect |
|---|---|
| **Deposit** | Accept into the current mailbox context, continue applicable processing into child branches, and bypass sibling branches. It avoids hard-coding a destination, permitting reuse. |
| **Transfer** | Move to an explicit destination and terminate further filtering, including later actions in the same filter. |
| **Copy** | Copy to an explicit destination and continue processing. |

For example, a reusable “keep” rule could be attached under several mailing-list mailboxes, each depositing into its own local archive context. **Re-Apply Filters** made read state, labels and replies useful for subsequent housekeeping. Outgoing filters ran too, but a Transfer did not automatically initiate a second distributed pass at its destination. [Manual pp. 99–102, 111–114](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [Porter’s incoming workflow, 2002-09-23](https://tidbits.com/2002/09/23/mailsmith-and-distributed-filtering-part-1/); [outgoing workflow, 2002-09-30](https://tidbits.com/2002/09/30/mailsmith-and-distributed-filtering-part-2/).

Criteria included known senders or address-book groups; synthetic **Server Account** information; primary/any recipients and any address; header text/body; label/priority/date/status; on-server, partial-message and spam status. Actions included Deposit/Transfer/Copy, Set Label, Set Priority, Play Sound, template-file replies to sender/all with send-or-queue behavior, **Set Subject permanently**, SpamCop reporting, notification, AppleScript, opening a message/mailbox, extracting enclosure copies and Stop Filtering This Message. The manual says there are **21 actions**, but the supplied text does not enumerate every menu entry; a complete 21-name list remains **unverified**. The archived vendor also documents printing as an action. [Manual pp. 103–110](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [archived filtering page](https://web.archive.org/web/20030620135905id_/http://www.barebones.com:80/products/mailsmith/filtering.shtml).

Rules could be applied manually, including an individually selected filter not ordinarily attached there. XML export/import supported reuse and backup; the XML was described as readable but not intended for hand editing. Filter-action logging helped troubleshooting. Later 2.3 notes distinguish a repaired **Skip Remaining Filters** action, which stops additional filters on the current mailbox, from stopping the entire message’s filtering. [Manual pp. 111–116](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [2.3 fixes](http://www.mailsmith.org/support/mailsmith/arch_notes_23.html).

#### Spam, scripts, clippings and application control

**SpamSieve integration is verified.** Mailsmith offered training/correction commands and classification before normal filtering. Automatically moving spam to spam/trash could bypass ordinary filters; leaving it in incoming enabled spam-aware rules. SpamAssassin/bogofilter headers were another input. An optional **Run Unix Command on Each Received Message** transformation ran earlier in the pipeline, taking/returning complete CRLF-formatted message data; failure or empty output preserved the original. This did not promise preservation of the original after a successful rewrite. [Manual pp. 83–85, 164](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

The older “Glossary” terminology evolved into **Clippings**: text files, nested sets and aliases, universal items, and automatic account-name-sensitive sets. Users could make a clipping from selected text, insert via completion/palette/shortcut, or Option-open it for editing. Expansion read the saved file, not an unsaved editor buffer. Stationery separately provided whole-message starting points, including `#quotedtext#`, `#unquoted-text#` and `#attribution#`. [Manual pp. 23, 177–184](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [glossary help](codex-artifacts/sources/help/glossary/glossary_using.html).

Clipping placeholders included clipboard, selection, file insertion, indentation, cursor/selection positions, name, local/GMT formatted date/time, timezone and UUID: `#CLIPBOARD#`, `#SELECT#`, `#FILE#`, `#INDENT#`, `#INLINE#`, `#INSERTION#`, `#SELSTART#`/`#SELEND#`, `#NAME#`, `#DATE#`, `#DATETIME …#`, `#DATETIME_GMT …#`, `#TIME#`, `#TIMEZONE#`, `#UUID#`. **`#SCRIPT …#` and `#SYSTEM …#`** inserted script-produced output. Nested expansion was powerful enough that the manual warned about recursive loops. **BBEdit Text Factories as a Mailsmith feature are unverified**; clippings, transformations, scripts and compatible plug-ins are the established mechanisms. [Manual pp. 180–195](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Automation went beyond “run a script.” Mailsmith exposed an OSA/AppleScript object model, **recordability**, scriptable searches, and scripts attached to filters. `FilterMessage(theMessage)` processed the current message; `ErrorLogged` responded to errors. Menu scripts named by menu/command could implement **`MenuSelect` before a command**, veto it, and **`PostMenuSelect` afterward**. Startup/shutdown items, script palettes, shortcuts and compatible BBEdit plug-ins expanded the workflow. The manual specifies compatibility with many **BBEdit 8.5-or-later-era** plug-ins, not today’s arbitrary BBEdit extensions. [Manual pp. 21–23, 110, 170, 185–195](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Customization included keyboard assignments for menus/scripts/clippings/stationery; hiding menu commands; contextual-menu categories; saved window geometry and stacking; palettes snapping to other palettes or screen edges; **32 labels** comprising eight fixed and 24 customizable entries; mail/filter/rebuild logs; quiet time, per-mailbox notifications, sounds/scripts, Dock alerts and then-current Growl/PowerMate integration. Printing could include headers, path, line numbers, timestamps and binding margins. [Manual pp. 14, 165–175](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

#### Files, migration and recovery

User data lived by default under **`~/Mail/Mailsmith User Data/`**. Its Mail package contained internal files; account settings used a plist, signatures XML, and filters separate files. Preferences included `com.barebones.mailsmith.plist`; support directories held scripts, clippings, plug-ins, stationery and sounds. The manual warned against manipulating Mail internals and supported relocation with a Finder alias. [Manual pp. 20–24](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

Import supported **mbox**, Pegasus **PMM**, Eudora, Claris Emailer and Apple Mail, with different coverage for addresses/settings. Import was non-destructive, could require approximately twice the original disk space, and preserved mailbox hierarchy where supported. Dragging a mailbox/message to Finder or using Export Mail produced **mbox**. Filters had XML interchange. These are useful migration exits; they do not establish that mbox exports include private notes, labels, rules or settings. [Manual pp. 18–26, 80, 115–116](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

The 2.2 upgrade offered backup before an incompatible database rebuild. **Master Password was removed**, and Apple Address Book became the only address book; older help still describes the former password and internal contacts. Do not mistake those help pages for 2.3.1 capabilities. [Manual pp. 18–19, 26](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [older application help](codex-artifacts/sources/help/preferences/application.html).

Two safety qualifications are especially consequential. First, decrypted PGP content was **cached as plaintext**, with encrypted storage recommended. Second, the source viewer’s “exactly as received” wording conflicts with a later statement that stored line endings are normalized to **CR**. Byte-for-byte original preservation therefore remains **unverified**. [Manual pp. 76, 78, 121](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

### 2.3 What users loved—and what “felt good” actually meant

**Contemporary reviews and practitioner accounts:**

- **Michael Tsai, ATPM, January 1999, reviewing 1.1.2:** appreciated a dense, nonmodal interface, keyboard control, query flexibility and the BBEdit-style editor. His account also found database operations markedly slower than competing clients. This is evidence for productive interaction despite backend costs, not uniformly fast performance. [Review](https://atpm.com/5.01/page8.shtml).
- **William Porter, TidBITS, 2002-07-15, reviewing 1.5:** valued plaintext composition, a better editor and unusually capable filtering/search. His experience was that 1.5 resolved earlier performance problems sufficiently to become his preferred client. In his September filtering articles, reusable mailbox-context rules let him manage mailing-list retention and file sent correspondence alongside received mail. [1.5 review](https://tidbits.com/2002/07/15/mailsmith-1-5-lean-mean-email-machine/); [Part 1](https://tidbits.com/2002/09/23/mailsmith-and-distributed-filtering-part-1/); [Part 2](https://tidbits.com/2002/09/30/mailsmith-and-distributed-filtering-part-2/).
- **Matt Neuburg, TidBITS, 2003-07-28:** centered his enthusiasm on text editing, scriptability and filtering. He described 2.0 as **“peppy, clean, crisp, and totally reliable”**, after reporting crashes and some data loss with 1.5. His favorable 2.0 experience cannot establish a reliability guarantee. [Switcher account](https://tidbits.com/2003/07/28/true-confessions-of-a-mailsmith-switcher/).
- **Andy Ihnatko, Macworld, 2003-10-31:** praised sophisticated editing, broad scripting and reusable filters, but found the interface inelegant and storage still slow. He worried that send failures could be missed in the errors mailbox. The editorial power and the execution defects coexisted. [2.0 review](https://www.macworld.com/article/168357/mailsmith2.html).
- **William Porter, MacTech, March 2004:** emphasized extensive settings and keyboard assignment, explaining that he used the mouse very little. The appeal was control over recurring work, rather than a fashionable visual design. [2.1 review](http://preserve.mactech.com/articles/mactech/Vol.20/20.03/Mailsmith2.1/index.html).
- **John Gruber, 2004-02-26:** described sticking with Mailsmith from 1.0 because it felt and scripted like BBEdit. His useful caution was **Command-E**: Use Selection for Find in BBEdit, Send Now in Mailsmith. A menu script intercepted sending when text was selected, preventing the habitual mistake without confirming every send. Gruber is credited in the manual, so this is a knowledgeable participant’s account. [Send Now Defense](https://daringfireball.net/2004/02/send_now_defense); [Manual p. 2](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

**Later recollection:** Fleishman’s **2016-06-27** account preferred a legible, user-chosen text font, compact mailbox display and precise scoped/regex queries, despite slow indexing and no threading. An HN participant on **2023-05-11** remembered Mailsmith as BBEdit for mail, while acknowledging that modern HTML correspondence complicated the proposition. These are personal preferences, not evidence that plaintext suits everyone. [Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/228272/in-defense-of-old-mac-software.html); [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=35904320).

A Reddit commenter on **2026-02-09** recalled:

> A workhorse. Not beautiful but efficient.

That captures a sentiment, not a benchmark or a measured market demand. [Reddit discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/SparkMail/comments/1r06hig/best_highvolume_email_client/).

**Community:** Porter described Mailsmith-Talk as active and helpful in 2002. The vendor later directed support to the Google group; its accessible archive includes Siegel’s 2015 release announcement and 2023 Gmail discussions. This verifies continuity of discussion, but not a current membership size or guaranteed support response. [Porter](https://tidbits.com/2002/09/23/mailsmith-and-distributed-filtering-part-1/); [vendor discussion page](http://www.mailsmith.org/discuss/); [Mailsmith Talk archive, checked 2026-10-11](https://groups.google.com/g/mailsmith-talk).

**Interpretation:** the attraction was cumulative fluency. Users could build muscle memory, reuse their own words and rules, inspect details, and gradually teach the application their workflow. “Native” included keyboard conventions, editable menus, multiple useful windows and automation—not merely widget appearance.

### 2.4 Dislikes and limits, with version boundaries

The strongest documented limitations were **POP-only retrieval**, plaintext-only composition and no integrated HTML rendering. Fleishman also reported absent threading in 2016. These are consequential constraints for synchronized, visually complex correspondence; they were welcomed simplifications for some users. [Manual pp. 13, 77](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [2016 retrospective](https://www.macworld.com/article/228272/in-defense-of-old-mac-software.html).

Performance and safety were uneven across versions. Tsai’s 1999 test reported slower storage, increased disk requirements and crash-related corruption concerns. Neuburg reported a much better 2.0 experience; Ihnatko still criticized speed. Search **expressiveness** should therefore be distinguished from search **latency**. [ATPM](https://atpm.com/5.01/page8.shtml); [TidBITS](https://tidbits.com/2003/07/28/true-confessions-of-a-mailsmith-switcher/); [Macworld](https://www.macworld.com/article/168357/mailsmith2.html).

Older reviews’ missing as-you-type spelling is **not a timeless Mailsmith limitation**: it is documented in the 2.2 manual. Likewise, early address-book and column limitations should not override later Contacts integration and configurable columns. Old help’s Word Services spelling integration belongs to the historical inventory, not the latest documented spelling model. [Manual pp. 26, 64–66, 74](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt); [older spelling help](codex-artifacts/sources/help/preferences/spelling.html).

The most direct recovery warning comes from **2.3, 2010-12-26**: **Reconstruct Mailboxes** recreated databases from message files while losing notes, labels, read/replied state and script-made changes; deleted attachments could reappear. The same notes fixed exclusion of the central Post Office database from Time Machine. This is a concrete reason to treat metadata and backup consistency as first-class requirements. [2.3 release notes](http://www.mailsmith.org/support/mailsmith/arch_notes_23.html).

Other documented costs were an initially unfamiliar filter model, implicit alphabetical execution, linear Boolean criteria, uneditable queued messages and dangerous regexes. A modern recreation should preserve capability while simplifying the explanation and protecting responsiveness. [Manual pp. 44, 94–105, 152](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).

### 2.6 Re-imagining the worthwhile features for 2026

The following is **proposed Sweetbriar behavior**, not a statement about implemented software. “Client-only” means no special server extension is required; ordinary downloading, sending or synchronization may still require the account service.

| Feature to revive | Modern native behavior | Server dependency and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| **Text transformation toolbox** | A searchable command palette for rewrap, quote depth, case, indentation, whitespace, append-copy and Paste Quoted; clipboard history with explicit selection. | **Client-only.** Rust owns transformations; native UI owns selection, accessibility and undo. |
| **Split/technical editing** | Optional split composer, line numbers, invisibles and rectangular mode; clearly indicate when column edits require disabling soft wrap. | **Client-only.** Keep these optional so ordinary composition stays simple. |
| **Plain-text/Markdown writing** | Choose plaintext or Markdown with preview; quote selected passages and preserve code/preformatted blocks during reflow. | Generation is local; transmission uses the account’s submission mechanism. Implement flowed text according to **RFC 3676**, including quote depth and `DelSp`, rather than treating screen wrapping as wire formatting. [RFC](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc3676.html) |
| **Clippings and stationery** | Filesystem-backed snippet sets, account/identity activation, typed fields, selection/cursor placeholders and repeatable expansion; offer script providers separately. | **Client-only** until optional snippet synchronization. Unfilled fields and failed expansion should leave a recoverable draft. |
| **Precise search** | One query builder for saved views and rules; nested Boolean groups, field/address-component matching, literal/regex/fuzzy modes, incremental results and visible scope/completeness. | Local cached data needs no extension. IMAP SEARCH and JMAP Email/query can supplement it; uncached bodies require retrieval, and server semantics must not be represented as identical. [IMAP RFC 9051](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9051.html); [JMAP RFC 8621](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8621.html) |
| **Contextual filters** | Reusable rules attached to logical folders or views, explicit evaluation order, branch acceptance, stop/continue choices and a message-by-message explanation before bulk application. | Local execution is portable. Remote filing requires provider operations. Always-on filtering requires provider rule support; compile a supported subset to **Sieve** where available. [RFC 5228](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5228.html) |
| **Labels, notification policies and retention** | Unlimited local labels, per-context notification levels and reversible “keep/archive” workflows; separate received-mail and sent-mail triggers. | Translate remote state through capabilities: IMAP flags/keywords, JMAP keywords/mailbox membership, Graph categories. Local colors and notification preferences remain local. [IMAP](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9051.html); [JMAP](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8621.html); [Graph update API](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/message-update?view=graph-rest-1.0) |
| **Searchable private notes and subject aliases** | Notes and an optional display subject attached to an internal message ID; search both while retaining the original subject and bytes. | **Client-only** by default. Cross-device notes need a separate synchronization design. Graph permits subject changes only on drafts, illustrating why Set Subject must become a local overlay for received mail. [Graph](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/message-update?view=graph-rest-1.0) |
| **Automation and send guards** | A documented object model, Shortcuts/AppleScript on macOS, plus shared commands usable by Linux tools; named pre-send checks and after-action hooks. | Local hooks are client-side. Sending/filing requires account permissions. Gruber’s selected-text send guard is a practical precedent. [Account](https://daringfireball.net/2004/02/send_now_defense) |
| **Spam pipeline** | Preserve original bytes; classify, explain the score, correct/train, then apply explicit routing rules. Provide an optional SpamSieve adapter rather than assuming compatibility. | Local classification works only when Sweetbriar runs. Server spam rules work independently. On **2026-10-11**, SpamSieve lists Mailsmith as a legacy integration; Sweetbriar support is **unverified**. [Requirements](https://c-command.com/spamsieve/help/requirements) |
| **Raw source, digest reading and remote inspection** | Headers/raw bytes beside a safe rendered view; MIME digest children as navigable items; a remote inspector showing download/state problems. | Local parsing/display are client-side; server inspection uses each backend’s API. Inspection should initially be read-only, rather than reproducing POP Monitor’s easy permanent deletion. |
| **Message links and portable export** | Stable `sweetbriar:` links resolved through local IDs; export messages plus an explicit metadata/settings manifest, with verification on re-import. | Local links require no server support. Cross-device resolution needs identity mapping/synchronized metadata. IMAP identities must account for **UIDVALIDITY**, not just message sequence numbers. [RFC 9051](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9051.html) |

An account provider should expose capabilities, not be forced into a POP-era folder abstraction. IMAP mailbox membership, JMAP’s multiple `mailboxIds`, and Graph’s update restrictions create real differences. **Inference:** treating a local view’s organization as a separate layer prevents a useful rule from demanding an impossible server mutation. [IMAP](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc9051.html); [JMAP](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8621.html); [Graph](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/message-update?view=graph-rest-1.0).

## 3. Options compared

| Decision | Option | Benefit | Cost / strongest objection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Composition | Strict plaintext, following Mailsmith | Predictable editing and simple output | Rejects too much visually structured correspondence. |
| Composition | **Plaintext + optional Markdown; safe HTML reading** | Preserves text fluency while offering a richer outgoing representation | Quoting HTML and round-tripping formatting require careful design. MailMate demonstrates this approach, not perfect interchange. [Manual](https://manual.mailmate-app.com/) |
| Composition | Fully rich-text-first | Familiar formatting controls | Harder to make rectangular edits, regex replacement and whitespace semantics coherent. This is a design trade-off, not an impossibility claim. |
| Rules | Reproduce Mailsmith semantics literally | Potential migration fidelity | Alphabetical order and subtle Transfer behavior remain surprising. |
| Rules | Flat ordered rules only | Familiar model and straightforward execution | Loses contextual reuse and visible hierarchy. |
| Rules | **Explicit contextual rules with preview** | Retains reuse while exposing control flow | More UI and a larger state model; must justify itself through real workflows. |
| Execution | Local rules only | Cross-provider capability and rich actions | Work stops when the client is unavailable. |
| Execution | Server rules only | Runs independently of the Mac | Provider-specific actions cannot reproduce arbitrary local scripts/filesystem operations. Sieve intentionally restricts execution. [RFC 5228](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5228.html) |
| Execution | **Local engine plus documented server subset** | Shared core with selective always-on behavior | Must prevent duplicate actions and explain where each rule runs. |

**Recommended choices are bold.** The counterargument to the overall combination is substantial implementation scope: editor fidelity, synchronization and two rule-execution environments could each become a project of their own. Build the shared model first, then add contextual sophistication incrementally.

## 4. Recommendations for Sweetbriar

### 4.1 Put reusable behavior in Rust; keep native interaction native

Use **AppKit `NSTextView` with TextKit 2** as the first composer prototype, with SwiftUI where useful for surrounding controls. Apple’s current documentation identifies `NSTextLayoutManager`, `NSTextContentStorage` and viewport layout, and recommends the modern layout engine for performance and international-language support. That establishes a relevant native foundation, **not automatic support for every BBEdit behavior**. Check rectangular selection, split views, IME, bidirectional text and accessibility in a focused prototype before committing. [Apple TextKit documentation, checked 2026-10-11](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/appkit/textkit).

Rust should own MIME-derived text, quote-aware transformations, snippet expansion, query/rule representation, storage and provider operations. Swift should own responder-chain behavior, selection, menus, windows, accessibility and undo presentation. Define explicit text-range mappings across the boundary. The future Linux UI can reuse transformations and commands while adopting its own toolkit’s interaction conventions.

**UniFFI** is a concrete binding candidate: its current documentation describes production-quality Swift generation, mapping Rust objects, records, enums, errors and bytes into Swift counterparts. Keep calls asynchronous at the UI boundary and exchange stable IDs and bounded result batches. No crate versions are selected by this report. [UniFFI Swift guide, checked 2026-10-11](https://mozilla.github.io/uniffi-rs/latest/swift/overview.html).

**Strongest counterargument:** a sophisticated custom editor can consume the project before mail synchronization works. Start with quote-aware rewrap, selection quotation, snippets, explicit wrapping and useful clipboard history; defer rectangular mode and elaborate split layouts until the native editor prototype proves sound.

### 4.2 Unify search, saved views and rules without reviving search freezes

Represent conditions once as a versioned Rust query tree: Boolean groups, field comparisons, address parts and text predicates. Compile it into local evaluation, candidate retrieval and a supported provider subset. Display whether results include all mail, cached mail only, or still-downloading bodies.

Candidate crates checked **2026-10-11**:

- **`tantivy`** for a derived full-text index, ranking and candidate retrieval; **`rusqlite`** for authoritative metadata and transactions. Tantivy’s index commit is a separate operation, so the two stores must not be assumed atomically consistent. [Tantivy](https://docs.rs/tantivy/latest/tantivy/); [rusqlite](https://docs.rs/rusqlite/latest/rusqlite/).
- **`regex`** for the default pattern mode. Its documented worst-case search bound is **O(m × n)**; it omits backreferences and lookaround. It cannot silently stand in for Mailsmith’s PCRE syntax. Bound pattern size, work and result volume too. [Crate documentation](https://docs.rs/crate/regex/latest/source/src/lib.rs).
- **`pcre2`** for an optional advanced compatibility mode. Its wrapper does not expose every PCRE2 capability; verify whether required execution limits are available, extend the boundary if needed, and use a killable worker for patterns that cannot be safely bounded. [Binding documentation](https://docs.rs/pcre2/latest/pcre2/).

Use exact post-filtering over candidates when an index cannot preserve a predicate’s semantics. A regex that cannot be safely narrowed may require a cancellable scan. Distinguish typo tolerance, token similarity and raw-text approximate matching; do not market them as interchangeable “fuzzy search.”

**Strongest counterargument:** a split index/database system adds failure modes. Start by benchmarking a simpler storage/search configuration against real archives; add Tantivy only when its latency and ranking justify index synchronization and recovery work. It remains a rebuildable accelerator, never the sole copy of mail or annotations.

### 4.3 Make safety an explicit persistence contract

Store fetched originals and outbound message versions immutably; keep parsed views and search indexes derived. Draft text, attachment references, private notes, labels, settings and operation history are **authoritative user data**, not disposable caches. For imports, preserve the original archive and report unsupported metadata rather than silently dropping it.

If using SQLite WAL, choose and test durability settings deliberately. SQLite documents that **`synchronous=FULL` syncs the WAL on each transaction commit**, whereas NORMAL omits that sync. Also, a current issue matters: its official documentation says the WAL-reset corruption bug was fixed in **3.51.3, released 2026-03-13**, with backports **3.44.6 and 3.50.7**. Pin a maintained, patched build and check the actually linked SQLite version. [SQLite WAL documentation, checked 2026-10-11](https://sqlite.org/wal.html).

Use a consistent backup operation for the database and a manifest tying it to message/attachment files; verify restores. SQLite’s online backup API is a concrete database snapshot mechanism. A copied database file alone must not be treated as proof that all Sweetbriar data was captured. [SQLite backup API](https://sqlite.org/backup.html).

Before transmission, durably save the draft/outbound version and operation state. Distinguish queued, submitting, accepted and uncertain outcomes. An interrupted SMTP final response can produce duplicate deliveries if blindly retried; RFC 5321 expressly discusses that ambiguity. “Undo Send” should mean cancellation before submission, not a universal recall promise. [RFC 5321 §4.5.3.2.6](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5321.html).

Proposed acceptance checks: kill the process during draft saves and filing; simulate disk-full conditions; interrupt submission before/after acceptance; rebuild the index; migrate and restore an archive containing notes, deleted attachments and settings. Verify both message bytes and metadata. Absolute protection against every hardware/provider failure is unattainable; the actionable goal is durable local commits, recoverable versions and explicit uncertain outcomes.

**Strongest counterargument:** durable saves and retained versions consume I/O and storage. Measure and batch safe operations, but never trade an acknowledged draft save or irreplaceable annotation for a misleadingly fast UI.

### 4.4 Preserve control while exposing consequences

Offer readable, versioned rule/snippet exports, command search, shortcuts and a small documented automation API. Show a rule’s execution location, order, affected messages and destructive effects. Keep received-message originals unchanged: subject edits, temporary reflow and annotations become overlays. Repeated sync events must not trigger repeated auto-replies, attachment exports or duplicate copies.

For reading, provide legible plaintext and a contained HTML alternative with explicit remote-content loading. Preserve quoted HTML when required rather than pretending text conversion is lossless. Keep a durable unsent queue that can be reopened as a new editable version, and put failed/uncertain sends where users will see them.

**Strongest counterargument:** power features can turn routine email into configuration work. Ship sensible defaults, a small visible command set and progressive disclosure. The first release should prove account synchronization, draft safety, responsive search and ordinary reading/replying before importing the entire classic-client toolbox.

## 5. Risks and open questions

- **Documentation versus runtime:** the manual is 2.2, the help book older, and release notes extend through 2.4.3. The manual’s apparent BBEdit carryovers—including a reference to versions before 6.5—must not become invented Mailsmith history. The full 21 filter-action names, byte-exact storage and Text Factories remain unverified. [Manual pp. 109, 121, 136](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).
- **Migration fidelity:** mbox is a useful content exit, but annotations, state and settings need separate export. Investigate AppleScript-assisted metadata extraction on a compatible old system before promising a complete Mailsmith importer. Do not perform recovery experiments against a user’s only archive. [Manual pp. 80, 185–193](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).
- **Provider differences:** confirm identities, remote filing, labels, offline search completeness and server-rule subsets separately for IMAP, JMAP, Gmail API and Exchange. Microsoft 365/Exchange and Proton integration policy research belongs in their own backend assignments; Mailsmith supplies no evidence of support for them.
- **Automation and privacy:** retain originals before successful preprocessing; make script/file access visible; prevent accidental transmission of notes or unresolved placeholders. Do not reproduce plaintext decrypted caches or old quarantine-disabling preferences as defaults. [Manual pp. 39, 78, 164, 184](codex-artifacts/sources/mailsmith-manual.txt).
- **Current gaps need direct testing:** the comparison establishes documented overlaps and explicit restrictions, not comprehensive absence. Test current client editors before making exclusivity claims. SpamSieve integration for Sweetbriar requires coordination and proof; historical Mailsmith support does not grant it automatically. [SpamSieve requirements](https://c-command.com/spamsieve/help/requirements).
- **Demand and prioritization:** enthusiastic reviews and recollections identify valuable workflows but do not measure willingness to adopt or pay in 2026. Validate quote editing, precise search and contextual rules with real archives and users before expanding the toolbox.
